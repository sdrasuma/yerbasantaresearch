Bio

Why Yerba Santa?

I became interested in yerba santa while looking into which plants native to California, and especially the Bay Area and San Francisco, are being used in biotech research. Its main neuroprotective compound, sterubin, is already well studied, but much of the plant's chemistry is not. I want to understand that overlooked chemistry: the non-flavonoid compounds and essential oils, and how they vary from one population to the next.

Species gallery

The Eriodictyon species

The genus Eriodictyon has ten species, most native to California. They look alike, but differ in leaf shape and hairiness, range and rarity. Five are rare or endangered in California.

Safety

Yerba santa is generally considered non-toxic, with no reports of significant toxicity from topical or internal use (Drugs.com). The exception is Eriodictyon parryi, poodle-dog bush, a potent skin irritant. Wear gloves and long sleeves around it in the field.

Research protocol · draft for mentor review

Comparing yerba santa chemistry across Bay Area populations

Built from my sources: the extraction and antioxidant methods of Richards & Chaurasia (2021), and the sampling, essential oil and polyphenol methods of Shock et al. (2026), applied to the comparisons in my research notes.

How to read this protocol

Each step is tagged with where it comes from:

Richards & Chaurasia 2021 Shock et al. 2026 My notes Standard practice (not in my sources)

Tier 1 uses a spectrophotometer and basic glassware, which most teaching labs have. Tier 2 needs GC-MS and UHPLC, usually through a core facility or collaborator.

Study design

Question, hypothesis and variables

Research question

Do wild populations of Eriodictyon californicum differ in their flavonoid and non-flavonoid chemistry, essential oils, antioxidant activity and leaf traits?

My notes

Hypothesis

Shock et al. found that essential oil components varied widely between cultivated accessions, suggesting chemotypes. Wild populations growing under different conditions should show the same or greater variation.

Shock et al. My notes

Independent variable

Population (site). Pick sites that differ in one main condition from my notes: stress (sun, drought), soil (e.g. serpentine) or habitat.

My notes

Dependent variables

  • Leaf thickness
  • Phytochemical classes present
  • Total phenolics and total flavonoids
  • DPPH, FRAP, superoxide and hydroxyl radical activity
  • Essential oil yield and composition
  • Individual polyphenols

Controls and reference

  • Ascorbic acid as the standard/positive control Richards
  • Dried leaf from Monterey Bay Spice Co., the source used by Richards & Chaurasia, run alongside your samples so your numbers can be compared with theirs
  • Solvent blanks Standard

Replication

Independent experiments in triplicate (n = 3), reported as mean ± SD Richards. Use several plants per site (Shock et al. compared 10 accessions) Shock et al.

Procedure

Step by step

  1. Preparation and permits

    Tier 1Standard practice
    • Find a faculty mentor and confirm which instruments you can use.
    • Locate populations with Calflora and iNaturalist; confirm identity with Jepson eFlora.
    • Get a collecting permit for public land, or written permission for private land.
    • Complete lab safety training.

  2. Field sampling

    Tier 1Shock et al.My notes
    • Harvest the plant tops (up to 20 cm of foliage) after flowering, as Shock et al. did in late October. Shock et al.
    • Freeze fresh samples at −20 °C and keep them frozen until processing. Shock et al.
    • Keep a second portion dried for the ethanol extracts, since Richards & Chaurasia worked with dried leaves. Richards
    • Sample sites that contrast in stress, soil or habitat; record signs of deer or insect damage. My notes
    • Sample all sites in the same week; record GPS, soil and life stage; keep one pressed voucher per site. Standard

  3. Leaf thickness

    Tier 1My notes
    • Measure fresh leaf thickness with a digital micrometer, avoiding the midrib, on several leaves per plant. Method: standard
    • Later, test whether thicker leaves have more phenolics or oil.

  4. Qualitative phytochemical screen

    Tier 1Richards
    • Test the ethanol extract for saponins, phlobatannins, phenols, tannins, terpenoids, cardiac glycosides, steroids and flavonoids using standard color and precipitation tests (their ref. 7).
    • Richards & Chaurasia found all eight classes. Check whether every population has them.

  5. Ethanol extraction

    Tier 1Richards
    • Prepare an ethanol extract of the dried, ground leaves.
    • Make a 1.0 mg/mL test solution, the concentration at which they report their results, plus a dilution series.
    • Take the ethanol strength, leaf-to-solvent ratio and extraction time from the full paper so your extracts match theirs.

  6. Total phenolics and flavonoids

    Tier 1Richards
    • Total phenolics: Folin–Ciocalteu method with gallic acid as standard; report µg GAE/mg.
    • Total flavonoids: aluminum chloride method with quercetin as standard; report µg QE/mg.

  8. Essential oil extraction and analysis

    Tier 2Shock et al.
    • Chop the fresh/frozen foliage.
    • Hydrodistill for 6 h in a Likens–Nickerson apparatus with continuous extraction of the distillate into dichloromethane.
    • Evaporate the dichloromethane under a stream of dry air and weigh the oil on an analytical balance to get yield.
    • Analyze by GC-MS and enantioselective GC-MS (their refs. 54–55).
    • Pay most attention to α-pinene, β-phellandrene and 1,8-cineole, the components that varied most and point to chemotypes; also record terpinen-4-ol and β-pinene.

  9. Polyphenol profiling

    Tier 2Shock et al.
    • Extract the foliage with methanol.
    • Identify compounds by UHPLC/ESI/Q-ToF mass spectrometry and quantify by UHPLC/DAD (their ref. 9).
    • Prepare standard stock solutions at 5 mg/mL in methanol for calibration curves (hesperidin needs a few drops of DMSO to dissolve).
    • Target the major compounds they found: eriolic acid C, homoeriodictyol, rosmarinic acid, sterubin, hesperetin, 6-methoxynaringenin and eriodictyol. Report as mg/g.
    • This step separates flavonoids (sterubin, eriodictyol, etc.) from non-flavonoids (eriolic acid C, rosmarinic acid), the gap in my notes. My notes

  10. Data analysis

    Tier 1RichardsShock et al.
    • Report mean ± SD of independent triplicates and compare sites with one-way ANOVA. Richards & Chaurasia used Excel 2010. Richards
    • Run hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) on the major oil components (average > 1%) and the polyphenol concentrations, to see whether plants group by site into chemotypes. Shock et al.
    • Add a post-hoc test (e.g. Tukey) to find which sites differ, and correlate leaf thickness with chemistry. Standard

Neuroprotection

Fischer et al. (2019) and Liang & Maher (2022) tested sterubin in nerve-cell models. Testing extracts from your highest-sterubin populations would need a cell culture lab and a collaborator.

Yerba santaEriodictyon californicum (Hook. & Arn.) Torr.

A California native shrub at the center of current biotech interest. Most research has focused on its flavonoids, especially sterubin. I'm looking at what has been overlooked: the non-flavonoid compounds and essential oils, and how they vary between plants.

Why this plant

Researchers at the Salk Institute identified sterubin in yerba santa as a potent neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory compound, which made the plant a candidate source for Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative disease research. A 2026 study of cultivated plants (Shock et al.) then found essential oil profiles that differ widely between plants, which hints at distinct chemotypes. If plants that look alike carry different chemistry, one species could supply a range of useful compounds.

Botany

The plant

Leaves

Lance-shaped and leathery. The upper surface is dark green with a glossy, sticky resin; the underside is net-veined with pale felt between the veins. Leaf thickness may differ between populations and species, which I want to measure.

Flowers

Tubular to funnel-shaped, white to pale lavender, in coiled clusters at the branch tips.

Fire ecology

Spreads by rhizomes and resprouts after fire, so it often dominates recently burned chaparral.

Classification

Formerly in Hydrophyllaceae. Now placed in Boraginaceae, or in a separate family, Namaceae, depending on the source.

Phenology

Ethnobotany

Uses & history

Several California Native American tribes used the plant for colds, upper respiratory infections (including whooping cough, pneumonia and tuberculosis), asthma and rheumatism, and applied it externally to sores. Spanish settlers adopted it too and named it yerba santa, "holy herb."

Atsugewi

Northern California. Chewed the plant for coughs and colds; used leaves and branches in a steam bath for rheumatism.

Miwok

Drank an infusion of the leaves and flowers for coughs and colds; applied a leaf poultice to aching or sore spots.

Tea and herb

Taken as a tea or eaten as an herb by Native Americans and Spanish settlers for headaches and respiratory and gastrointestinal problems.

Pharmacy

Later used in American pharmacy as an expectorant and to mask bitter medicines; still used as a flavoring in food and drinks.

Tribal uses as summarized in the Oregon cultivation study (its ref. 4). Trace them to the original ethnographic sources before citing.

Phytochemistry

Flavonoids and non-flavonoids

Flavonoids share a C6–C3–C6 carbon skeleton. Non-flavonoid polyphenols, such as phenolic acids, lack it. Essential oil components (terpenes) are a separate, volatile group. Most yerba santa research has looked only at the first column.

Flavonoids

Well studied
  • SterubinAntioxidant, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective; studied for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. The Salk team reports it can bind excess iron (Fischer et al., 2019). Liang & Maher (2022) studied which parts of its structure drive these effects.
  • EriodictyolAntioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, cardioprotective, hepatoprotective and anticancer activity reported.
  • HomoeriodictyolAntinociceptive (pain-reducing) in animal models; also a known bitter-taste masker.
  • HesperetinAntioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective and antimicrobial effects reported.
  • 6-MethoxynaringeninAmong the major polyphenols in the Oregon samples.

Non-flavonoids & essential oils

Research gap
  • Eriolic acid CThe most prominent polyphenol in the Oregon samples, yet little studied.
  • Rosmarinic acidPhenolic acid; antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and neuroprotective effects reported.
  • 1,8-CineoleEssential oil. Antibacterial against respiratory pathogens; mucolytic; anti-inflammatory in the airways. Varied widely between plants.
  • α-Pinene, β-pinene, β-phellandreneEssential oil. α-Pinene and β-phellandrene varied widely between samples; pinenes are antibacterial and β-pinene is antinociceptive.
  • Terpinen-4-olEssential oil. Antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity against Staphylococcus aureus.

The respiratory activity of the essential oil components fits the plant's traditional use for coughs and colds, which could be a strong angle for the proposal.

Chemical composition of Eriodictyon californicum (California yerba santa) cultivated in Ontario, Oregon, USA

According to the authors, the first study of the plant's essential oil. They analyzed 10 accessions grown from seed in a high-desert field.

Aims

  • Show it can be grown in a harsher high-desert climate
  • Check whether cultivated plants' polyphenol profiles match wild plants
  • Obtain and describe the essential oils

Methods

  • Seeds pre-treated for germination; transplanted June 2024 into silt loam; drip irrigation, hand weeding
  • Top 20 cm of post-flowering foliage harvested 24 & 28 Oct 2024; frozen at −20 °C
  • Likens–Nickerson hydrodistillation with dichloromethane extraction
  • Oils by GC-MS and enantioselective GC-MS; polyphenols extracted in methanol, identified by UHPLC/ESI/Q-ToF, quantified by UHPLC/DAD
  • Hierarchical cluster analysis and PCA

Findings

  • Main polyphenols: eriolic acid C, homoeriodictyol, rosmarinic acid, sterubin, hesperetin, 6-methoxynaringenin, eriodictyol
  • Rich in sterubin and eriolic acid C
  • α-Pinene, β-phellandrene and 1,8-cineole varied widely between plants, suggesting several chemotypes
  • Essential oils may add to the leaves' medicinal value

Limits to keep in mind: one cultivated site outside the native range and one harvest date. Wild populations, other life stages and Bay Area plants are untested.

Terms

Glossary

Polyphenols
Plant compounds with multiple phenolic groups; antioxidant, with roles in pigmentation and defense. Found in fruit, tea, coffee and herbs.
Flavonoids
Polyphenols with a C6–C3–C6 skeleton that give plants color and act as antioxidants. Sterubin and eriodictyol are examples.
Non-flavonoids
Polyphenols without the flavonoid skeleton: phenolic acids, stilbenes (resveratrol), lignans, ellagic acid.
Phenols
Compounds with one or more –OH groups attached to an aromatic ring.
Tannins
Astringent polyphenols that bind proteins. Two types: hydrolyzable and condensed.
Phlobatannins
A type of condensed tannin found in bark and leaves.
Saponins
Bitter plant glycosides that foam in water, as in soybeans, quinoa and licorice.
Terpenoids
Compounds built from five-carbon isoprene units; behind a plant's smell, color and defense. Main components of essential oils.
Cardiac glycosides
Compounds that strengthen heart contractions and affect heart rate, as in foxglove and oleander.
Steroids
Lipids with four fused carbon rings, involved in cell structure, growth and hormones.
Essential oil profile
The breakdown of the volatile compounds (monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, phenols, esters) in a plant's oil.
Chemotype
Plants of one species that look alike but produce a different mix of chemicals.
Oxidative stress
Free radicals building up faster than antioxidants clear them, damaging lipids, proteins and DNA.
Nutraceutical
A health product derived from food or plant compounds.
DPPH / FRAP
Lab assays that measure antioxidant power: radical scavenging and ferric reducing ability.
GAE / QE
Gallic acid and quercetin equivalents; units for total phenolic and flavonoid content.
